# SUAS 2028: Competition Aircraft Design and Program Plan

**Target:** RoboNation Student Unmanned Aerial Systems (SUAS) competition, September 2028, Skyway Range, Tulsa, Oklahoma.

**Status:** Concept design, v0. Sizing below is preliminary and carries the assumptions marked "(unv)". Rules cited are from the 2026 team handbook; RoboNation revises the handbook annually, so every point value must be re-verified against the 2028 rules when they publish.

This is the club's first competition program. It runs alongside, not instead of, the [Part 103 piloted vehicle](../vehicle/baseline.md) line.

---

## 1. Why this competition, and why 2028

SUAS is annual, airframe-agnostic, drivable from Austin (~450 miles), and its mission skeleton — autonomous waypoint laps, mapping, search and delivery — is stable from year to year even as the theme changes. No other UT Austin organization currently enters it.

**2028, not 2027.** A September 2027 entry would require a flying, autonomous aircraft by July 2027, before the club has members, money, or a single flown airframe. Competing in 2028 gives two full academic years: one to build the team and the fundamentals, one to build and prove the aircraft. See [section 7](#7-timeline) for what happens in the gap year, including scouting the 2027 event in person.

## 2. Reading the rubric before drawing the aircraft

SUAS is scored out of **1250 points**. The design below is shaped by where those points actually are.

| Category | Points | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Technical Design Report | 150 | 10 pages, IEEE format. Competition Strategy, Design Strategy, Testing Strategy are 37.5 each |
| Team website | 100 | |
| Flight endurance | 250 | 20 for takeoff/landing, 30 if both autonomous, plus **200 × (laps/10)²** |
| **Efficient Operators** | **200** | **200 × min(1, (4−O)/2)**, where O = operator count. Two operators scores full marks, three scores 100, four scores zero |
| **Design for Rapid Response** | **200** | Weight **50 × min(1, (35−W)/20)** · packing tier **50/25/10** · toolless assembly **25** · every battery under 100 Wh **75** |
| Search, detect, and deliver | 200 | Two objects, 100 each: survives 20, within 50 ft 50, correct target 30 |
| Risk mapping | 150 | |

Three structural facts drive everything:

1. **650 of 1250 points do not require flying well.** Documentation (250), operator count (200), and the Design for Rapid Response properties (200) are won at the design table and the writing desk. A disciplined team banks most of this before it takes off.
2. **Endurance is quadratic, and the clock is 30 minutes.** Laps are ~2 miles each, up to 10, so a maximum score means **20 miles of autonomous flight inside a 30-minute mission clock** that also has to contain takeoff, the mapping run, and both deliveries. Five laps earns 50 of 200 points, not 100. **Speed, not battery capacity, is the binding constraint** — 20 miles in 30 minutes leaves no room for anything else, so the realistic target is 5 to 7 laps flown fast, and the aircraft is sized for cruise speed.
3. **Autonomy gates the rest.** Laps must be flown autonomously and in order before mapping or delivery may be attempted, and deliveries attempted before a completed lap score zero.

## 3. Configuration choice

| | Multirotor at 6.8 kg | **Lift-plus-cruise quadplane at 6.8 kg** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Practical cruise speed | 15 m/s | **23 m/s** |
| Lap miles flyable inside a 30-minute clock | ~6 (3 laps) | **~12 to 14 (6 to 7 laps)** |
| Cruise power | ~600 W | **~250 W** |
| Endurance points | 3 laps → 18 + 30 = **48** | 6 to 7 laps → 72 to 98 + 30 = **102 to 128** |
| Packing tier | Poor: 18 in props | Carry-on with a multi-piece wing |
| Weight term | Achievable | Achievable, tighter |

**Decision: lift-plus-cruise quadplane.** Four lifting rotors on booms for vertical takeoff, landing, and precision hover over delivery targets; one pusher propeller and a fixed wing for the laps. This is also what the recent winners fly — Embry-Riddle took first in 2025 with a fixed-wing VTOL, and Cornell's *Hermes* is described as a VTOL "engineered for rapid toolless deployment, extended range efficiency, and end-to-end autonomy," which is the rubric restated as a product description.

A secondary benefit: lift-plus-cruise is the architecture of real eVTOL aircraft (Beta ALIA, Wisk Gen 6). It moves the club toward the industry, not away from it. The coaxial multirotor line continues separately, aimed at Part 103 and heavy-lift work.

## 4. Vehicle concept, v0

| Parameter | Value | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Configuration | Lift-plus-cruise quadplane: 4 lift rotors, 1 pusher, fixed wing, V-tail | Endurance term plus hover precision for delivery |
| All-up weight target | **6.8 kg (15.0 lb)** | Weight term saturates at 15 lb; every pound above costs 2.5 points |
| Wing | ~1.9 to 2.1 m span, ~0.45 to 0.55 m², AR 8, **4-piece** | Higher wing loading than a loiter design, because the mission rewards speed; 4 sections fit the carry-on tier |
| Cruise speed | **22 to 25 m/s** | Mission clock is 30 minutes; laps are speed-limited, not energy-limited. Above ~25 m/s the 150 ft turn-radius limit demands >50 deg bank and waypoint tolerance suffers |
| Lift rotors | 4 × ~13 in folding props on booms | Hover ~1.0 kW total, used only in takeoff, landing, and delivery |
| Energy | **4 × ~88 Wh Li-ion packs (352 Wh total)** | Each pack under the 100 Wh rule: 75 points |
| Flight controller | Pixhawk-class, commercial | Do not put club-designed hardware in the flight-critical path |
| Companion computer | Jetson Orin Nano class | Detection and classification onboard |
| Payload | **Both delivery objects carried simultaneously**, each with a retardant descent device | Reloading is prohibited; freefall delivery is explicitly not successful |
| Assembly | Toolless, under 3 minutes, ≤4 people | 25 points, and it must be designed in from the first CAD session |

### Mass budget (v0, preliminary)

| Group | Mass (g) |
| --- | --- |
| Battery packs (4 × 88 Wh, Li-ion 21700) | 1,900 |
| Airframe: wing, booms, fuselage, tail | 1,600 |
| Lift propulsion: 4 × motor, ESC, folding prop | 800 |
| Cruise propulsion: motor, ESC, prop | 350 |
| Avionics: flight controller, GNSS, radios, wiring | 400 |
| Companion computer, camera, mount | 500 |
| Payload release mechanism | 200 |
| Delivery payloads | 600 |
| Fasteners, connectors, margin | 450 |
| **Total** | **6,800 g (15.0 lb)** |

The budget closes exactly at the scoring threshold, which means **weight is the binding constraint on the whole program**. Every gram of scope creep costs points directly. (unv: masses are estimates until parts are selected.)

### Time budget: the real currency (30-minute mission clock)

| Phase | Time (min) | Energy (Wh) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Takeoff and transition | 1.5 | 25 |
| 6 laps, 12 miles at 23 m/s | 14.0 | 58 |
| Risk mapping run | 4.0 | 18 |
| Detection and two delivery runs | 6.0 | 60 |
| Landing and margin | 2.0 | 25 |
| **Total** | **27.5 of 30** | **186** |
| Usable energy from 352 Wh at 85% | | 300 |
| **Reserve** | **2.5 min** | **114 Wh (38%)** |

The aircraft runs out of clock long before it runs out of battery. That inverts the usual instinct: **do not add battery capacity, add speed and cut turnaround time.** Extra cells cost the weight term twice, in points and in cruise power, and buy nothing the clock will let you use.

At 6 laps, one more lap is worth about 25 points and takes about 2.3 minutes, so **a minute of saved mission time is worth roughly 10 points.** The places to find minutes: transition time, the mapping pattern, and lining up each delivery.

## 4.1 What the aircraft must actually do

Compiled from the 2026 team handbook. Re-verify against the 2028 handbook when it publishes.

### A. Compliance: fail any of these and you do not fly

| Requirement | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Weight | All-up flying weight **35 lb or less** |
| Propulsion | **Battery electric only.** No fuel |
| Flight | Heavier-than-air, free-flying, untethered |
| Registration | Registered on FAADroneZone, certificate presented at inspection, **registration number displayed on an external surface** |
| Remote ID | Broadcasting unique vehicle ID and position |
| Safety pilot | Holds **TRUST**, the Recreational UAS Safety Test |
| Manual override | Safety pilot can take manual control **at any time** |
| Failsafes | Autonomous **return-to-home or return-to-land**, and autonomous **flight termination**. Both the safety pilot and the GCS operator must be able to trigger them |
| Disarm | **Software disarm is not sufficient** - physical power disconnection required. Personnel clear of the propeller arc whenever motors can receive power |
| Airframe integrity | **No pieces may depart the aircraft in flight**, except the delivery components |
| Batteries | **Brightly colored** for crash visibility, removable **without deconstructing the aircraft**, **no swaps during the mission clock**, specs and manufacturer MSDS submitted with hard copies on site, LiPo safe bags, daily inspection for swelling or venting, charging monitored at all times |
| Design lock | **One design, locked at Proof of Flight Readiness submission.** Backup airframes allowed for development only |
| Ground equipment | Nothing over **15 ft**. Safety functions must run on onsite systems, with **no internet or cloud dependency** |
| RF | FCC-compliant only, must not connect to other teams' systems. Intentional jamming is cheating |
| Traffic | Prepared to avoid other UAS, since **up to two fly simultaneously** |
| Environment | Operate in **~14 mph average wind** and temperatures to **110 F**, secure equipment quickly in precipitation, minimum 3-mile visibility |

### B. Qualification: the July gate

A video of **5 minutes or less** with team identification and a safety, risks and mitigations segment, plus an autonomous flight demonstrating:

- takeoff to **above 50 ft AGL**
- **1+ mile of autonomous flight at full loaded weight, in a single flight**
- waypoints hit within **100 ft**
- **turn radius under 150 ft**, with climb and descent angles up to **20 degrees**
- landing

A separate **Proof of Pilot Competence** video is also required. Miss these and the season ends in July.

### C. The mission, inside 30 minutes

| Task | What the aircraft must do |
| --- | --- |
| **Waypoint laps** | Up to 10 laps of about 2 miles, up to 15 waypoints each, flown **in order and fully autonomously**, within 100 ft of each waypoint. Partial laps score nothing |
| **Risk mapping** | Produce a stitched aerial map of the area of interest, PNG or JPEG named with the team name, **handed to judges on a USB drive inside the 30 minutes**. Judged on coverage, projection accuracy and stitching. Noticeable stitch errors or exposure variation drop it to medium quality; poor quality scores zero |
| **Search, detect, deliver** | **Carry both objects at once** - a **GP908 strobing beacon, about 155 g**, for the tent, and an **8 oz water bottle, about 255 g, 2 to 2.5 in diameter and 5 to 6 in tall**, for the mannequin. Landing to reload is prohibited. Detect both targets autonomously from aerial imagery, a pop-up tent in the open state and a mannequin in any orientation among debris, and deliver the correct object to each |
| **Delivery constraints** | Release **above the 150 ft AGL altitude floor**, each payload **2 lb or less**, no flight-sustaining mechanism, must land undamaged and be safe to retrieve, **freefall delivery is explicitly not successful so a retardant device is required**, and **at least one complete autonomous lap must be flown before any delivery** |
| **Interoperability** | Pull mission data from the judges' server, push telemetry at **1 Hz or better while airborne**, and consume other teams' telemetry for avoidance |

### D. The hardest requirement, stated plainly

**Land a payload within 50 ft of a target, released from at least 150 ft AGL, in 14 mph wind.**

Run the numbers. From 45.7 m, a payload under a conventional parachute descending at 3 m/s is in the air for 15 s, and a 6.3 m/s wind carries it **94 m, about 308 ft - six times the scoring radius**. Even an aggressive drogue descending at 9 m/s drifts about 32 m, or 105 ft, and still misses.

The 50-point accuracy bonus therefore cannot be won by the mechanism alone. It needs three things together:

1. a **fast-descending retardant device**, a drogue or streamer rather than a full canopy, to cut exposure time while still satisfying "not freefall" and "lands undamaged";
2. a **wind-corrected release point** computed from the autopilot in-flight wind estimate, which is a ballistic drop solution rather than releasing when overhead;
3. a **drop-test campaign** that measures real descent rate and drift and calibrates the release solution against data.

A steerable parafoil, the obvious way to guide a payload down, most likely runs into the "no flight-sustaining mechanism" rule. The legal path is a dumb, fast, high-drag payload released at a computed point.

This is a mechanism-and-software co-design problem worth up to 100 points across the two deliveries, and **it can be developed and tested entirely on the existing 1/3-scale multirotor** long before the quadplane exists.

## 5. Differentiating factors

The competition is not won by a more exotic airframe. It is won by taking the categories most teams treat as paperwork.

**1. Battery architecture as a scoring subsystem — 75 points most teams forfeit.**
Every battery onboard must be under 100 Wh. A team that buys one large LiPo pack loses all 75 points and cannot recover them at the competition. Four club-designed ~88 Wh Li-ion modules take the full 75, and Li-ion at ~250 Wh/kg instead of LiPo at ~180 Wh/kg buys back roughly 600 g, which feeds the weight term and the endurance term at the same time. **One subsystem, three scoring categories.** This is the club's existing expertise — the same independent-pack architecture, pack switch board, and BMS work already designed for the Part 103 vehicle.

**2. A two-operator aircraft, designed as such from day one — 200 points.**
The safety pilot and the ground station operator must do everything, and neither may perform any other task in flight. That forbids a human spotter for the drop, a separate camera operator, or an extra person loading payloads between tasks. This is an architecture decision made in the first month or not at all: preloaded payloads, one-button mission execution, onboard detection with no human in the loop.

**3. Weight discipline as a stated requirement, not an aspiration — 50 points.**
15.0 lb is a hard design requirement with a mass budget tracked from week one, reviewed at every design review, the way flight hardware programs actually work.

**4. Toolless deployment engineered, not retrofitted — up to 75 points across packing and assembly.**
A four-piece wing with captive quarter-turn fasteners, blind-mate electrical connectors on the booms, and folding props, all designed together. Retrofitting toolless assembly onto a finished aircraft does not work; it has to constrain the structural layout from the start.

**5. Documentation as an engineering deliverable — 250 points.**
The report is 10 pages of IEEE format with Competition Strategy, Design Strategy, and Testing Strategy at 37.5 points each. Every one of those sections rewards evidence: trade studies, test data, and the reasoning connecting them. The club already writes this way.

**6. Interoperability treated as a first-class subsystem.**
Teams must pull mission data from the judges' server and push telemetry at 1 Hz or better while airborne, and consume other teams' telemetry for obstacle avoidance. It is unglamorous, it is required, and rookie teams underbuild it.

## 6. What to take from the competition, and what to improve

### Take

| From | What | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Embry-Riddle (1st, 2025) | Fixed-wing VTOL configuration | The endurance term rewards it decisively |
| Embry-Riddle | **Commercial flight controller, deliberately** | They chose a Pixhawk Cube so effort went to "system integration, mission logic and operational testing" rather than reinventing flight control. A club that designs its own boards must hear this |
| Cornell CUAir | Airdrop treated as its own engineering subsystem with an owner | 200 points depend on payload survival and 50 ft accuracy |
| Cornell CUAir | "Rapid toolless deployment" as a design driver, not a feature | It is 75 points |
| KFUPM (winner 2024) | Standing subteams with faculty advisors, year-round | The visible differentiator is iteration across years, not a novel gadget |
| Purdue PART | 50+ members across aero, mech, EE, and software | Scale of team matches scope of mission |

### Improve

| Gap we can exploit | Our approach |
| --- | --- |
| Most teams buy off-the-shelf packs and lose the 100 Wh points | Club-designed sub-100 Wh Li-ion modules; 75 points plus weight and endurance |
| Teams staff missions with 3 to 4 operators | Two-operator architecture from the first requirements document; +100 to +200 points |
| Autonomy bolted on late, forcing manual fallback that forfeits lap credit | Autonomy stack running in simulation before the airframe exists |
| Weight managed by hope | Mass budget under configuration control from week one |
| Reports written in the last month | Report written continuously; testing evidence collected as it is generated |

The field is not soft: 81 teams from 19 countries entered in 2025, up from 36 in 2024, and 56 advanced. Beating the median means beating serious international programs.

## 7. Timeline

Two academic years. The competition is mid-September 2028; the real deadline is the **flight readiness video in July 2028**, which must show a 1+ mile autonomous flight at full loaded weight, waypoints within 100 ft, turn radius ≤150 ft, and climb/descent within 20°.

### Year 1 — build the team and the fundamentals

| When | Milestone |
| --- | --- |
| Fall 2026 | Recruit and onboard. Two officers earn **FAA Part 107**. Study the current handbook end to end. Requirements document and configuration trade study |
| Fall 2026 | Fundraising: sponsorship packet, department and student government funding, HornRaiser campaign |
| Spring 2027 | **Conceptual design review.** Autonomy stack flying waypoint missions in simulation. First club-designed ~88 Wh pack module on the bench. Power board v1 |
| Spring 2027 | Buy or build a cheap trainer quadplane; learn transitions with hardware nobody cries over |
| Summer 2027 | Detection pipeline running on the companion computer against recorded aerial imagery. Payload release mechanism drop-tested from a tower or a multirotor |
| **Sept 2027** | **Attend SUAS 2027 in Tulsa as volunteers or spectators.** Walk the course, watch the judging, meet the teams, photograph everything. This costs a tank of gas and is worth a semester of guessing |

### Year 2 — build and prove the competition aircraft

| When | Milestone |
| --- | --- |
| Fall 2027 | **Preliminary design review** against the published 2028 rules. Structures fabrication begins. Full avionics integration on the bench |
| Fall 2027 | First hover of the competition airframe. Mass budget audit against the 15.0 lb requirement |
| Dec 2027 | **Critical design review.** Report outline written; website live |
| Jan – Mar 2028 | Transition flights. Autonomous waypoint laps. Registration opens around December; **register by ~1 April 2028** |
| Mar – May 2028 | 20-mile endurance validation. Delivery accuracy campaign to 50 ft. Mapping pipeline validated. Interop client tested against the judges' reference server |
| May – Jun 2028 | **Two-operator mission rehearsals**, full dress, timed, with nobody else allowed to touch the aircraft |
| **Jul 2028** | **Flight readiness video, pilot competence video, technical design report, team website submitted** |
| Aug 2028 | Spare airframe complete. Repair kit, transport cases, contingency drills |
| **Sept 2028** | Compete, Tulsa |

### Should we also enter 2027?

Optional and defensible: enter with a modified commercial VTOL airframe purely to pass the flight readiness gate and learn the submission process, with no expectation of a competitive score. It costs a registration fee, a few thousand dollars of hardware, and a summer. The alternative — scouting the 2027 event in person and putting everything into the 2028 aircraft — is cheaper and is the baseline plan here. Decide by January 2027.
